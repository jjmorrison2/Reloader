The reloading process, step by step

This is the general sequence for metallic-cartridge handloading. Your press and die instructions take precedence for equipment specifics. Work with one powder on the bench at a time, straight from its original, labeled container.

  1. Inspect the brass. Discard cases with splits, neck cracks, a bright ring ahead of the case head (incipient case-head separation), crushed mouths, or loose primer pockets. Sort by headstamp for best consistency.
  2. Clean the cases (tumbler, wet pin, or ultrasonic) so dirt doesn't scratch your dies and you can see defects.
  3. Lubricate and resize / deprime. Bottleneck rifle cases need case lube (stuck cases are no fun); straight-wall pistol cases with carbide dies don't. Full-length size for autoloaders; neck sizing is a bolt-gun-only option.
  4. Check case length — trim if needed. Compare to the maximum case length for your cartridge; trim to the trim-to length, then chamfer and deburr the mouth. Rifle brass stretches and needs this regularly; pistol brass rarely does.
  5. Prime. Seat the primer type your load data specifies, anvil-side in, just below flush. A high primer can cause misfires or slam-fires. Note some brass runs a different primer size than typical for the cartridge (e.g. small-primer .45 ACP and some 6.5 Creedmoor brass) — check your headstamps.
  6. Charge with powder. Set your measure, then verify the charge on a scale — check the first several throws and spot-check continuously (every charge for max-effort or small-capacity loads). Charge cases in a loading block and visually inspect every case with a light before seating bullets: identical powder height in every case. This is your double-charge / no-charge (squib) check.
  7. Seat the bullet to the cartridge overall length (COL/COAL) shown in the data. Seating deeper than published raises pressure — especially in small pistol cases.
  8. Crimp appropriately. Taper crimp for auto-pistol rounds that headspace on the case mouth (9mm, .45 ACP); roll crimp for revolver rounds with a cannelure (.38 Special); most bottleneck rifle rounds need only neck tension.
  9. Gauge every round. Case gauge or barrel "plunk test" for pistol; check COAL and function. Look for bulges, high primers, shaved jackets.
  10. Label the box with cartridge, bullet, powder and charge, primer, brass, COAL, and date. Unlabeled handloads are hazardous waste.

Working up a load (the ladder)

  1. Load 5–10 rounds at each charge step the calculator's ladder shows, starting at the published starting charge — never at the calculated target.
  2. Fire the lowest charge first. Inspect every fired case and primer before moving to the next step up.
  3. Chronograph each step. Published velocities came from the publisher's test barrel; yours will differ. If your velocity reaches your target — or your measured velocity runs well above the published number for that charge — stop there.
  4. If you see any pressure sign (see Safety tab), stop, back off, and pull any remaining higher-charge rounds.

What this tool does — and doesn't

  • It only interpolates linearly between a published starting load and a published maximum load from powder-manufacturer data. It contains no internal-ballistics model, and it will never suggest a charge above a published maximum or below a published start.
  • The real charge-to-velocity curve is not perfectly linear, and your barrel is not the test barrel. The estimated charge is a planning number for your work-up ladder, not a load to jump to. The chronograph and your fired brass are the truth.
  • Published data applies to the exact components tested — that bullet, that case, that primer, that COAL. Changing any component means going back to the starting charge. If your exact bullet isn't listed, get data for it from a published manual rather than assuming same-weight data transfers.
  • This library was seeded from powder manufacturers' free published data, with each entry linked to its source. Transcription errors are possible anywhere — including here — which is why every recipe card carries its source link. Check it.
  • Personal use only. The publishers (notably Hodgdon) license their data for personal, non-commercial use. Use this tool privately; don't republish, redistribute, or bundle its built-in data into anything you share.

About the rifle & conditions adjustments

  • The barrel-length and temperature inputs apply rule-of-thumb estimates (default 25 fps per inch of barrel, 1.0 fps per °F vs a 70°F reference) to the velocity you should expect — they never change the published charge limits. Real barrels vary a lot; only a chronograph tells you your actual velocity.
  • Powder temperature sensitivity varies widely: "Extreme"-line powders (H4350, Varget, H4895) and Vihtavuori powders run much lower than 1 fps/°F; ball powders often run higher. Adjust the fps/°F figure to your powder, or set it to 0 and rely on the chronograph.
  • Heat raises pressure. A load worked up in winter can show pressure signs (or worse) on a 100°F day, especially near max. Re-verify warm-weather performance, and keep extra margin below max on loads you'll shoot hot.
  • Twist rate is recorded for reference only: it affects bullet stability (whether a long/heavy bullet flies point-first), not charge or velocity. Check the bullet manufacturer's minimum twist for your bullet.

Pressure signs — stop if you see any

  • Flattened, cratered, pierced, or leaking primers (soot around the primer).
  • Shiny swipe or ejector-hole mark on the case head; extractor gouges.
  • Hard extraction, sticky bolt lift, or a sluggish/violent change in cycling.
  • Case-head expansion; primer pockets loose after one or two firings.
  • Chronograph velocity significantly above the published figure for that charge.

Low-charge dangers (why "just load it light" is not safe either)

  • Below-start charges can lodge a bullet in the bore (squib). A weak report or soft recoil means stop immediately and check the bore before firing again.
  • Large cases with very small charges of slow powder can behave unpredictably. Never go below the published starting charge; if you want less velocity than the start load produces, choose a different (faster) powder or lighter bullet with published data in that range.

Bench rules

  • One powder container on the bench at a time; return it to the container when done. Never store powder in the measure.
  • No distractions while charging. If you're interrupted, dump the block and start over.
  • Wear eye protection when priming and shooting. Store powder and primers separately, cool and dry.
